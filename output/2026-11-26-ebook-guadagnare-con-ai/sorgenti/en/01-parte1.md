# Understanding Artificial Intelligence {.part}

Before you can earn money with a tool, you need to understand it. This part explains, without technical jargon, what artificial intelligence tools are, how they work, which ones matter most, how to give them effective instructions and what their limits are. It's the foundation for everything else in this book.

# What Artificial Intelligence Is and How It's Changing Work {.chapter}

## A simple definition

**Artificial intelligence** (**AI** for short) refers to a set of technologies that allow a computer to perform tasks that, until recently, required human intelligence: understanding a text, writing a new one, recognizing what's in a photo, translating, summarizing, answering questions, generating images or voices.

AI doesn't "think" like a person. It has no intentions, emotions or consciousness. It's a very sophisticated statistical system that has learned from enormous numbers of examples and produces plausible responses based on what it has learned. This distinction isn't philosophical: it has huge practical consequences, because it explains both why AI is so useful and why it sometimes gets things wrong with great confidence.

## Three families of AI you'll encounter

In everyday language the word "AI" is used for very different things. To find your way around, you only need to distinguish three families.

| Family | What it does | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|
| Predictive AI | Analyzes data and makes predictions or classifications | Spam filters, purchase recommendations, card fraud detection |
| Perceptive AI | Recognizes images, sounds, voice | Unlocking your phone with your face, voice dictation, image search |
| Generative AI | Creates new content: text, images, audio, video, code | ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Midjourney, voice and video generators |

This book focuses on **generative AI**, because it's what has made it possible for anyone, even without technical skills, to produce in little time content and services that used to require many hours of specialized work.

## What has really changed

Artificial intelligence has existed for decades. What has changed in recent years is that it has become:

1. **Accessible.** You use it by writing in natural language, without programming.
2. **Affordable.** Many tools have a free plan; paid plans for individual use cost, at the end of 2026, generally between $5 and $25 a month.
3. **Versatile.** The same tool can help you write an email, analyze a spreadsheet, prepare a marketing plan and edit a text.
4. **Multimodal.** The latest models understand and produce not only text but also images, audio and documents.

The result is that the **production cost** of much digital content (texts, images, video drafts, presentations) has dropped dramatically. This creates opportunities, but also a consequence many people ignore: **when something becomes easy for everyone to produce, its value moves elsewhere**. The value is no longer in producing just any text, but in knowing *which* text is needed, *for whom*, *why*, and in guaranteeing its quality.

## How the world of work is changing

Rather than replacing entire professions, AI is changing the mix of tasks within each profession. Let's look at some concrete examples.

- A **social media manager** used to spend much of their time drafting captions and searching for ideas. Today they can generate ten variations in a few minutes and spend the time saved on strategy, the client relationship and analyzing results.
- A **small shop owner** who couldn't afford a designer can now create a decent flyer, a product description and a polite reply to a negative review on their own.
- A **translator** no longer translates every sentence from scratch, but reviews and adapts pre-translated texts, focusing on tone, terminology and quality control.
- A **private tutor** can prepare personalized exercises for each student in a fraction of the time.

In all these cases three trends emerge that will run through this whole book:

1. **Repetitive work shrinks.** Standard tasks (first drafts, variations, summaries, formatting) take much less time.
2. **Human judgment is worth more.** Choosing, checking, adapting to context and taking responsibility become the skills clients pay for.
3. **New services emerge.** Many small businesses want to use AI but don't have time to learn: people who know how can offer setup, training and management.

::: {.box .tip}
**Tip.** When you evaluate an idea for making money, always ask yourself: "If tomorrow everyone could do this with AI in five minutes, would my service still be valuable?" If the answer is yes, because you add knowledge, care, relationships or responsibility, the idea is solid. If the answer is no, you're selling something that's bound to lose value quickly.
:::

## The myth of "passive income"

Plenty of online content presents AI as a machine that makes money by itself: "create a hundred ebooks in a day and earn while you sleep." It's important to dismantle this idea right away, because it's the main cause of beginners' disappointments and financial losses.

- Digital marketplaces (Amazon, Etsy, social media) are already full of mass-produced, low-quality content. Platforms are responding with limits, checks and stricter rules: Amazon KDP, for example, asks you to disclose AI-generated content, and in October 2026 Etsy banned the sale of AI-generated "functional guides" (recipes, patterns, instructions).
- Even a digital product, once created, needs updates, customer support, promotion and administration.
- What can become *partly* passive is the income from a **good** product, in a **well-chosen niche**, supported by a **promotion channel** that works. Getting there takes active work at the start.

In this book, then, we'll talk about **scalable income** (a product you can sell many times without remaking it) and **service income** (paid work for a client), with realistic expectations for both.

## The skills that matter

To make money with AI you don't need advanced technical skills. You need these five abilities instead, all of which can be learned:

| Skill | Why it matters | Where you develop it in this book |
|---|---|---|
| Giving clear instructions | The quality of the result depends on how you ask | Chapter 4 and Part 6 |
| Judging quality | AI also produces convincing errors | Chapter 6 and every example |
| Understanding a client's problem | People pay for a solution, not a text | Part 4 |
| Presenting and selling | Without sales there's no business | Part 2 and Part 4 |
| Organization and consistency | Results come over time | Part 7 |

::: {.recap}
### What we learned
- Generative AI creates text, images, audio and video from instructions in natural language.
- It doesn't think like a person: it produces plausible responses based on what it has learned.
- It has reduced the production cost of content; value is shifting to choice, verification, relationships and responsibility.
- "Passive income" without work is a myth: scalable income and service income both exist, and both require upfront effort.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Believing people who promise fast, guaranteed income.
- Focusing on activities anyone can replicate in five minutes.
- Thinking you need technical skills to get started.

# How Generative AI Works {.chapter}

You don't need to become an engineer to use AI well, just as you don't need to understand an engine to drive a car. But knowing roughly how it works lets you get better results and predict when it will make mistakes.

## Language models in simple words

Tools like ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are based on **Large Language Models**, or **LLMs**.

A language model has been "trained" by reading an enormous quantity of text: books, web pages, articles, code, documents. During training it learned one thing only, but to an extraordinary degree: **predicting which piece of text is most likely to come next**, given everything that came before.

When you type a question, the model doesn't "look up the answer" in an archive. **It builds the answer one small piece at a time**, each time choosing the most suitable piece based on context: your question, the instructions you gave, the previous conversation.

![How a language model builds an answer: it reads the context, calculates the most likely continuations and chooses one, one piece at a time.](immagini/diagramma-token-en.svg)

### What tokens are

Those "small pieces" are called **tokens**. A token can be a whole word, part of a word or a punctuation mark. In English, a word corresponds on average to a little more than one token. Tokens matter for two practical reasons:

1. **Memory limit.** Each model can consider a maximum number of tokens at a time (the so-called **context window**). Today this window is very large, but in very long conversations the model can "forget" or confuse the initial instructions.
2. **Cost.** When you use models through pay-as-you-go services, you pay according to the number of tokens processed.

## Why AI seems intelligent

To predict the next text well, the model had to learn a huge number of patterns: grammar, style, the most common facts, the structure of reasoning, how to write a complaint letter or a marketing plan. That's why it can perform very different tasks without having been specifically programmed for each one.

After initial training, models are further refined with the help of people who evaluate their answers, so that they become more useful, clearer and safer. It's this phase that makes them able to follow instructions and hold a conversation.

## Why AI gets things wrong with confidence

Now that you know the mechanism, you also understand its main flaw. The model produces what is **plausible**, not necessarily what is **true**. If it lacks reliable information on a topic, it still tends to produce an answer that looks credible. This phenomenon is called **hallucination**.

Typical examples of hallucinations:

- quotes attributed to people who never said them;
- titles of books or articles that don't exist;
- precise but invented statistics;
- laws described approximately or in outdated form;
- product prices and features that are no longer current.

::: {.box .warn}
**Warning.** Whenever AI-generated content contains a **verifiable fact** (a number, a date, a name, a law, a price, a quote), check it against a reliable source before using it, especially if the content will be sold or published on a client's behalf.
:::

Many tools today can **search the web** and cite sources. This reduces hallucinations but doesn't eliminate them: sources must also be opened and read, because the model can summarize them badly.

## The model's "knowledge date"

Every model has a **knowledge cutoff**: it knows the world well up to a certain point and little or nothing of what happened afterwards, unless it uses web search. If you ask for a service's current prices without enabling search, you might get information that's months out of date.

## How image, audio and video generators work

The principle is similar, even though the technique is different.

- **Images.** Image generators have been trained on huge numbers of "image + description" pairs. When you write a description, the system starts from an image made of random "noise" and gradually transforms it into an image consistent with the text. That's why two identical requests produce different images.
- **Voice and audio.** Voice models have learned the characteristics of the human voice (rhythm, intonation, timbre) and can read a text with very natural synthetic voices, or clone a voice from a sample, which requires **the consent of the person** whose voice it is.
- **Video.** Video generators produce short sequences from text or an image. In 2026 quality has improved a lot, but videos remain short (often a few seconds per generation), cost credits and require several attempts to get a usable result.

## What this means for you in practice

| How it works | Practical consequence | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The model predicts the most plausible text | Generic answers if the question is generic | Give context, examples and a precise objective |
| It doesn't distinguish true from plausible | Possible convincing errors | Check facts, numbers and sources |
| It has a knowledge cutoff | Information sometimes outdated | Use web search and check dates |
| Each generation is different | Variable results | Generate several variations and choose |
| It has a context window | In long chats it "forgets" | Summarize and start a new chat |

::: {.recap}
### What we learned
- Language models build answers one token at a time, predicting the most likely continuation.
- They seem intelligent because they have learned the patterns of language and reasoning.
- They produce what is plausible, not necessarily true: "hallucinations" are a structural risk.
- Image, voice and video generators follow similar logic and always produce different results.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Treating AI as an infallible search engine.
- Publishing numbers, quotes or laws without checking them.
- Continuing the same conversation endlessly when the model starts getting confused.

### Practical exercise
Open an AI chat tool on its free plan and ask: "List three books published on the topic of time management, with author and year." Then check each title in an online bookstore or library catalog. Note how many results are correct, how many have wrong details, and how many, if any, don't exist.

- [ ] I can explain what a token is.
- [ ] I know what a hallucination is and how to protect myself.
- [ ] I know that models have a knowledge cutoff.
- [ ] I've completed the book-title verification exercise.
:::

# ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and the Other Main Tools {.chapter}

There are hundreds of AI tools, but to start you only need a few. In this chapter you'll get to know general-purpose **AI assistants**, the tools you'll use every day to write, reason, research and plan. Specialized tools (images, video, voice, websites, automation) are covered in Part 3.

## What an AI assistant is

An AI assistant (or *chatbot*) is an application that lets you have a conversation with a language model. You type a request in a text box, get an answer, and can continue the conversation by asking for changes, more detail or new versions. The leading assistants today also let you:

- upload documents (PDFs, spreadsheets, presentations) and have them analyzed;
- upload images and ask for them to be described or for their text to be extracted;
- search the web for up-to-date information;
- generate images (some directly, others through connected tools);
- use your voice to speak and listen;
- create projects or workspaces with saved instructions and documents.

## The three main assistants

### ChatGPT (OpenAI)

This is the best-known assistant and the one through which many people discovered generative AI. It's versatile, has a simple interface, and integrates image generation, web search, file analysis and voice mode.

- **Strengths:** great versatility, a large ecosystem, built-in image generation, plenty of tutorials available.
- **Weaknesses:** usage limits on the free plan are reached quickly with intensive work; the default writing style tends to be recognizable and needs guiding.
- **Plans (checked November 2026, US prices):** free; *Go* $8/month (localized pricing in some countries); *Plus* $20/month; *Pro* from $100/month, with higher tiers.

### Gemini (Google)

This is Google's assistant, integrated with the Google ecosystem (Gmail, Docs, Drive, YouTube). It's especially convenient if you already work with Google tools.

- **Strengths:** integration with Google Workspace, good handling of long documents and multimedia content, a subscription that includes cloud storage.
- **Weaknesses:** some features vary from country to country; the plan lineup has changed several times.
- **Plans (checked November 2026):** free; *Google AI Plus* (listed at €4.99/month in some European countries); *Google AI Pro* ($19.99/month in the US; European prices vary by country); *Ultra* for heavy use.

### Claude (Anthropic)

This is the assistant developed by Anthropic, valued in particular for the quality of its writing, its ability to work on long documents and to follow detailed instructions.

- **Strengths:** natural, polished writing, analysis of long documents, close adherence to instructions, projects with reference documents.
- **Weaknesses:** usage limits on the free plan can be tight for long tasks.
- **Plans (checked November 2026, US prices):** free; *Pro* $20/month with monthly billing; *Max* $100/month or $200/month for very heavy use.

::: {.box .tip}
**Tip.** Don't subscribe to everything right away. For two weeks, use the free versions of at least two assistants for the same tasks. Then subscribe to **just one**, the one that gave you the best results for the kind of work you want to do. One subscription used well is worth more than three used halfway.
:::

## Other useful tools to know

| Tool | Type | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| Microsoft Copilot | Assistant | Integration with Word, Excel, PowerPoint and Outlook |
| Perplexity | Answer engine | Research with cited sources; Pro plan $20/month (third-party sources, November 2026) |
| Mistral Le Chat | Assistant | European alternative, useful if you prefer EU providers |
| NotebookLM (Google) | Assistant for your documents | Summarizing and querying only the documents you upload |
| DeepL | Translation and writing | High-quality translation and style editing |

## How to choose: comparison table

| Criterion | ChatGPT | Gemini | Claude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ease of use for beginners | Very high | High | High |
| Writing long, polished texts | Good | Good | Very good |
| Built-in image generation | Yes | Yes | No (external tools are used) |
| Integration with office tools | Good | Excellent with Google | Good via connectors |
| Web search with sources | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Free plan | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Basic individual subscription | $8–20/month | ~$20/month | $20/month |

The ratings in the table are indicative and based on practical use: models are updated often, and a tool that's weaker today may overtake the others in six months. That's why this book teaches **methods** that work with any assistant.

## Getting started, step by step

1. Go to the official website of the tool you've chosen (type the address yourself; don't click sponsored ads: there are clone sites that imitate famous tools).
2. Create an account with your email or a Google/Apple/Microsoft account.
3. Open the **settings** and look for the section on **data and privacy**. Consider turning off the use of your conversations to train models, if the option is available.
4. Look for the **custom instructions** section (or "personalization," "memory"): here you can write who you are and how you prefer answers. Example: "I'm a beginner. Answer in English, simply, with numbered steps when you explain a procedure."
5. Make your first request: "Explain in five points what you can do to help me organize my work week."

::: {.box .warn}
**Warning.** Never enter other people's personal data (clients, colleagues, family members), passwords, banking details or confidential documents into assistants, unless you're using a business plan with adequate contractual guarantees and you have the right to do so. We cover this in detail in the chapter on privacy.
:::

- ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude all have a free plan and an individual subscription of around $20 a month.

- Learning a method matters more than chasing the tool of the moment.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Subscribing to many tools before you know what you need.
- Signing in through sponsored links or unofficial sites.
- Entering personal or confidential data into assistants.

Friendly tone, maximum 120 words." Compare the two answers on three criteria: clarity, naturalness of tone, commercial usefulness. Give each criterion a score from 1 to 5.

# How to Write Effective Prompts {.chapter}

A **prompt** is the instruction you give to AI. It's the most important skill in this book: the same tool can produce a bland text or a professional one depending on how you ask.

## Why generic prompts give generic results

Remember how models work? They produce the most plausible continuation *given the context*. If the context is thin ("write an Instagram post for a shop"), the most plausible answer is an average post, just like a thousand others. If the context is rich (who the shop is, who it's for, what the post should achieve, what tone it uses, which examples it likes), the answer becomes specific and useful.

## The RCOFE method: five elements of a good prompt

So you don't forget anything, use this five-part framework. It isn't a magic formula: it's a checklist.

![The RCOFE method: Role, Context, Objective, Format, Examples & limits.](immagini/diagramma-prompt-en.svg)

| Element | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **R**ole | Who should the AI "be"? | "Act as an experienced copywriter for small local businesses." |
| **C**ontext | What's the situation? | "I own an artisan bakery in Bergamo; my customers are local families and nearby offices." |
| **O**bjective | What do I want to achieve? | "I want more orders for custom birthday cakes." |
| **F**ormat | What should the result look like? | "Three Instagram posts, maximum 80 words each, ending with a question." |
| **E**xamples & limits | What to imitate, what to avoid? | "Warm tone, no excessive emojis, don't mention prices. Here's a post I like: …" |

### From weak prompt to effective prompt

**Weak prompt:**

```text
Write a post for my bakery.
```

**Effective prompt:**

```text
Act as an experienced copywriter specializing in communication
for small local businesses.

Context: I own an artisan bakery in Bergamo. My customers are mostly
families from the neighborhood and employees from nearby offices.
My specialty is custom cakes made with seasonal ingredients.

Objective: increase requests for custom birthday cakes,
to be ordered at least 3 days in advance.

Format: write 3 Instagram posts, maximum 80 words each.
Each post must open with an attention-grabbing sentence
and end with an invitation to send us a direct message.

Limits: warm, friendly tone, maximum 2 emojis per post,
don't mention prices. Avoid clichés like "uncompromising quality."
```

The difference in the result is huge, and the extra time to write the second prompt is about two minutes.

## Six techniques that make a difference

### 1. Give concrete examples

If you have a text you like, paste it in and write: "Use this as a reference for tone and length; don't copy its content." Examples communicate style better than any adjective.

### 2. Ask before doing

For important work, add at the end of your prompt: "Before you start, ask me up to 5 questions to get any information you're missing." The AI will help you clarify what you hadn't specified.

### 3. Break big tasks into steps

Don't ask "write a 50-page ebook." First ask for the table of contents, then correct it, then ask for one chapter at a time, each time providing the approved table of contents and summaries of the previous chapters.

### 4. Ask for variations, then choose

"Suggest 10 different titles, numbered, in different styles: some direct, some intriguing, some with a number." Then: "Develop numbers 3 and 7." Choosing is faster than describing.

### 5. Have the result critiqued

"Now reread the text as a strict editor. List the 5 weakest points and suggest an improved version." This technique, called **self-critique**, greatly improves quality.

### 6. Say what you *don't* want

Models tend to use recurring formulas. Listing expressions to avoid ("in today's fast-paced world," "discover," "revolutionary," "in conclusion") makes the text more natural.

## Iterative prompting: you work in conversation

The first result is rarely the final one. The typical flow is:

1. **Initial prompt**, complete (using the RCOFE framework).
2. **Evaluation:** what works? What doesn't?
3. **Targeted correction:** "The second paragraph is too formal. Rewrite it as if you were talking to a friend; keep the information."
4. **Repeat** until you get the result.
5. **Final human review:** read everything, check the facts, fix the details by hand.

::: {.box .tip}
**Tip.** Create a document called "My prompts" and save the ones that work. In a few weeks you'll have a personal library that saves you many hours. Part 6 of this book gives you a starting base of more than fifty prompts.
:::

## Prompts for images: a different language

For image generators the framework changes. They work best with concrete visual descriptions organized like this:

1. **Subject:** what's in the image.
2. **Setting:** where it is.
3. **Style:** photography, illustration, watercolor, 3D, minimalist…
4. **Light and color:** soft morning light, pastel palette, strong contrast…
5. **Framing:** close-up, overhead, wide angle…
6. **Format:** square, vertical 4:5, horizontal 16:9.

Example:

```text
Overhead photograph of a light wooden desk with an open notebook,
a cup of coffee and a small plant. Soft natural morning light from
the left, neutral beige and sage green palette, minimalist style,
lots of empty space on the right for adding text.
Vertical 4:5 format.
```

::: {.recap}
### What we learned
- A prompt rich in context produces specific results; a vague prompt produces average results.
- The RCOFE method (Role, Context, Objective, Format, Examples & limits) is an effective checklist.
- Examples, preliminary questions, breaking work into steps, variations and self-critique greatly improve results.
- Images require visual descriptions: subject, setting, style, light, framing, format.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Settling for the first result.
- Asking for huge jobs in a single prompt.
- Not specifying tone, length and audience.
- Publishing the text without a human review.

### Practical exercise
Choose a real business you know (a shop in your neighborhood, a professional, your own company). Write a weak prompt and an RCOFE prompt to get a 100-word description for its Google profile. Run both and compare the results. Then apply the self-critique technique to the better result.

- [ ] I know the five elements of the RCOFE method.
- [ ] I've written at least one complete prompt.
- [ ] I've used the variations and self-critique techniques.
- [ ] I've created my "My prompts" document.
:::

# How to Use AI Without Technical Skills {.chapter}

Many people give up before they start because they think AI is "for computer people." In reality, today's tools are designed to be used by writing or speaking, just as you would with a collaborator. In this chapter we'll look at how to get organized and which everyday uses let you become comfortable in a few days.

## The minimum kit to get started

| What | Why | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| A computer or tablet with a keyboard | For long tasks, a phone is uncomfortable | What you already have |
| An up-to-date browser (Chrome, Edge, Safari, Firefox) | All the tools work in the browser | Free |
| An email address dedicated to work | To keep accounts and communications separate | Free |
| An AI assistant | Your main tool | Free to start |
| A design tool (for example Canva) | For polished images and documents | Free plan available |
| A storage system (Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox) | To save and organize files | Free plans available |
| A password manager | To protect the many accounts you'll create | Free options exist |

## Organize your digital workspace

Before producing anything, create a folder structure. It seems like a detail, but it'll save you hours of searching.

```text
AI-Work/
├── 01-Prompts/           (prompts that work)
├── 02-Projects/
│   └── ProjectName/
│       ├── research/
│       ├── drafts/
│       ├── final/
│       └── images/
├── 03-Clients/           (one subfolder per client)
├── 04-Admin/             (receipts, subscriptions, invoices)
└── 05-Ideas/
```

## Ten everyday uses to build confidence

The best way to learn is to use AI for real problems, small and frequent. Here are ten exercises to do over a week.

1. **Rewrite a difficult email.** "Rewrite this email so it's polite but firm: [text]."
2. **Summarize a long document.** Upload a PDF and ask: "Summarize it in 10 points, then list the deadlines and figures it contains."
3. **Make a plan.** "Help me organize a house move over 4 weeks. Create a table with week, task and person responsible."
4. **Explain a topic.** "Explain Italy's flat-rate tax regime (regime forfettario) as if I were 15, then tell me what questions to ask an accountant."
5. **Brainstorm.** "Give me 20 birthday gift ideas for someone who loves gardening, with a budget of €40."
6. **Edit a text.** "Correct the grammar and punctuation without changing the style. List the corrections you made."
7. **Translate and adapt.** "Translate this ad into English, adapting it for a British audience."
8. **Analyze a spreadsheet.** Upload a file of expenses and ask: "What are the three highest spending categories? Create a chart."
9. **Prepare for an interview or negotiation.** "Play a demanding client who wants a discount. I'm the supplier. Let's begin."
10. **Create a simple image.** "Create a square image for an invitation to a dinner with friends, illustrated style, warm colors."

## Using your voice

Almost every assistant has a **voice mode**. It's very useful if you type slowly or want to "think out loud": you can describe an idea while walking and then ask the assistant to turn it into an organized text. You can also dictate the context of a long prompt and then correct it in writing.

## Working with "projects" and saved instructions

The leading assistants let you create **projects** (or spaces, or custom assistants) where you save instructions and reference documents that apply to every conversation in that project. It's the most useful feature when you work continuously for a client or on a product.

Example of project instructions for a client:

```text
You are the communications assistant for Aurora Physical Therapy
(a fictional client). Audience: adults aged 35-65 with back and
neck pain, amateur athletes.
Tone: professional, reassuring, never alarmist.
Rules: don't diagnose, don't promise recovery, always invite people
to an in-clinic assessment. Use "you." No emojis in long texts.
Attached documents: service price list, FAQ, 5 posts the client
approved in the past (use them as a style reference).
```

## How much time you'll really save

Go back to the list of three activities you wrote in Chapter 1. For each one, try doing it with AI and measure the time, **including review**. Many people discover that:

- for first drafts, the savings are large;
- for texts that require precise information, the savings are smaller, because checking takes time;
- for high-quality creative work, AI speeds up the early stage, but the final polish remains human.

This personal measurement is more reliable than any general statistic: it tells you where AI really makes you more productive.

::: {.recap}
### What we learned
- To use AI, you only need to be able to write and use a browser.
- A free minimum kit is enough to get started.
- A tidy folder structure is the first professional step.
- Small, real everyday uses are the fastest way to learn.
- "Projects" with saved instructions are ideal for recurring clients and products.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Waiting to "understand everything" before you start.
- Working without organization and losing files and prompts.
- Measuring time saved without including review.

### Practical exercise

# Opportunities, Limits, Mistakes and Risks to Know {.chapter}

Before moving on to concrete ways to make money, you need an honest picture of what AI can and can't do, of beginners' most common mistakes and of the risks to manage. This chapter is an operational overview; Part 8 goes deeper into legal and security issues.

## The real opportunities

Generative AI creates opportunities in four directions:

1. **Doing what you already know faster.** If you're good at writing, teaching, organizing or selling, AI multiplies your productivity.
2. **Doing things you couldn't afford before.** Graphics, short videos, translations, simple websites: today a beginner can reach a decent level with inexpensive tools.
3. **Helping others use AI.** Many small businesses know they should use it but don't have time to learn. This creates demand for services.
4. **Creating digital products.** Guides, templates, courses and resources can be produced at low cost and sold many times, if they solve a real problem.

## The limits to accept

| Limit | What it means | How to manage it |
|---|---|---|
| Errors and hallucinations | Invented facts presented with confidence | Always check facts |
| Genericness | "Average" texts, similar to many others | Rich context, examples, revision in your own voice |
| No lived experience | AI hasn't tried what it talks about | Add your own experience, tests and real cases |
| Inconsistency | Different results with every attempt | Saved instructions, examples, final check |
| Limits with hands, text and details in images | Frequent visual defects | Check at full zoom, retouch |
| Platform dependence | Prices and rules change | Diversify tools and channels |

## Beginners' most common mistakes

1. **Flooding the market with generic products.** Publishing dozens of ebooks or prints without care leads to zero sales, negative reviews and sometimes account closure.
2. **Copying the competition.** Using AI to remake another seller's product is unfair, often illegal, and doesn't set you apart anyway.
3. **Underestimating promotion.** Creating the product is 30% of the work; getting it known is the rest.
4. **Prices that are too low.** Cutting prices to sell attracts the most difficult clients and makes the business unsustainable.
5. **Ignoring platform rules.** Every platform has rules on AI content, intellectual property and transparency: breaking them can cost you your account.
6. **Forgetting taxes and social contributions.** Even small earnings can have tax consequences.
7. **Chasing every new tool.** Switching tools every week stops you from getting good at any one of them.

## The main risks in brief

- **Legal:** copyright, using other people's trademarks, images of real people without consent, misleading advertising.
- **Privacy:** entering clients' personal data into AI tools without a legal basis and adequate safeguards.
- **Transparency:** in the European Union, since 2 August 2026 the transparency obligations of Article 50 of the EU Artificial Intelligence Regulation (AI Act) apply, covering, among other things, the labeling of *deepfakes* and certain generated content. We discuss this in Part 8.
- **Reputation:** an error published on a client's behalf damages both you and them.
- **Financial:** useless subscriptions, expensive courses sold with false promises, scams.

::: {.box .warn}
**Warning.** Be wary of anyone who sells you a "system" for making money with AI while showing only screenshots of income. A real business is judged by its products, clients, verifiable reviews and transparency about costs, not by screenshots of an account.
:::

## A matrix to help you choose among the 12 business models

In Part 2 we'll analyze twelve ways to make money. To get your bearings, here's how they compare by type of income and time needed to see the first results. The ratings are qualitative and indicative.

![Matrix of the 12 business models: services (faster income, tied to your time) and products (slower income, scalable).](immagini/diagramma-matrice-en.svg)

| Business model | Type | First income possible | Scalability |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ebooks | Product | Slow | High |
| Digital products and templates | Product | Slow | High |
| Etsy | Product | Medium | High |
| Amazon KDP | Product | Slow | High |
| Your own social content | Audience | Very slow | Very high |
| Social media management for businesses | Service | Fast | Low |
| Images and graphics | Service/Product | Medium | Medium |
| Promotional videos | Service | Medium | Low |
| Copywriting | Service | Fast | Low |
| Websites and landing pages | Service | Medium | Medium |
| Online courses | Product | Slow | High |
| AI services agency | Service | Medium | Medium |

**Rule of thumb:** services bring income sooner but require your time; products take longer to get going but can be sold many times. Many solid businesses start with a service (to earn and understand the market) and then create a product (to scale).

::: {.recap}
### What we learned
- The opportunities are real: productivity, new services, access to skills, digital products.
- AI's limits are structural and must be managed with verification, experience and revision.
- The most common mistakes are genericness, copying, low prices and lack of promotion.
- Services bring income sooner; products scale more but start more slowly.

### Mistakes to avoid
- Starting with the most "passive" product when you have no sales channel.
- Ignoring platform rules, privacy and taxes.
- Buying expensive courses based on income promises.
