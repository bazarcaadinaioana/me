Chapter 1

What Artificial Intelligence Is and How It’s Changing Work

A simple definition

Artificial intelligence (AI for short) refers to a set of technologies that allow a computer to perform tasks that, until recently, required human intelligence: understanding a text, writing a new one, recognizing what’s in a photo, translating, summarizing, answering questions, generating images or voices.

AI doesn’t “think” like a person. It has no intentions, emotions or consciousness. It’s a very sophisticated statistical system that has learned from enormous numbers of examples and produces plausible responses based on what it has learned. This distinction isn’t philosophical: it has huge practical consequences, because it explains both why AI is so useful and why it sometimes gets things wrong with great confidence.

Three families of AI you’ll encounter

In everyday language the word “AI” is used for very different things. To find your way around, you only need to distinguish three families.

Family What it does Everyday examples
Predictive AI Analyzes data and makes predictions or classifications Spam filters, purchase recommendations, card fraud detection
Perceptive AI Recognizes images, sounds, voice Unlocking your phone with your face, voice dictation, image search
Generative AI Creates new content: text, images, audio, video, code ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Midjourney, voice and video generators

This book focuses on generative AI, because it’s what has made it possible for anyone, even without technical skills, to produce in little time content and services that used to require many hours of specialized work.

What has really changed

Artificial intelligence has existed for decades. What has changed in recent years is that it has become:

  1. Accessible. You use it by writing in natural language, without programming.
  2. Affordable. Many tools have a free plan; paid plans for individual use cost, at the end of 2026, generally between $5 and $25 a month.
  3. Versatile. The same tool can help you write an email, analyze a spreadsheet, prepare a marketing plan and edit a text.
  4. Multimodal. The latest models understand and produce not only text but also images, audio and documents.

The result is that the production cost of much digital content (texts, images, video drafts, presentations) has dropped dramatically. This creates opportunities, but also a consequence many people ignore: when something becomes easy for everyone to produce, its value moves elsewhere. The value is no longer in producing just any text, but in knowing which text is needed, for whom, why, and in guaranteeing its quality.

How the world of work is changing

Rather than replacing entire professions, AI is changing the mix of tasks within each profession. Let’s look at some concrete examples.

  • A social media manager used to spend much of their time drafting captions and searching for ideas. Today they can generate ten variations in a few minutes and spend the time saved on strategy, the client relationship and analyzing results.
  • A small shop owner who couldn’t afford a designer can now create a decent flyer, a product description and a polite reply to a negative review on their own.
  • A translator no longer translates every sentence from scratch, but reviews and adapts pre-translated texts, focusing on tone, terminology and quality control.
  • A private tutor can prepare personalized exercises for each student in a fraction of the time.

In all these cases three trends emerge that will run through this whole book:

  1. Repetitive work shrinks. Standard tasks (first drafts, variations, summaries, formatting) take much less time.
  2. Human judgment is worth more. Choosing, checking, adapting to context and taking responsibility become the skills clients pay for.
  3. New services emerge. Many small businesses want to use AI but don’t have time to learn: people who know how can offer setup, training and management.

Tip. When you evaluate an idea for making money, always ask yourself: “If tomorrow everyone could do this with AI in five minutes, would my service still be valuable?” If the answer is yes, because you add knowledge, care, relationships or responsibility, the idea is solid. If the answer is no, you’re selling something that’s bound to lose value quickly.

The myth of “passive income”

Plenty of online content presents AI as a machine that makes money by itself: “create a hundred ebooks in a day and earn while you sleep.” It’s important to dismantle this idea right away, because it’s the main cause of beginners’ disappointments and financial losses.

  • Digital marketplaces (Amazon, Etsy, social media) are already full of mass-produced, low-quality content. Platforms are responding with limits, checks and stricter rules: Amazon KDP, for example, asks you to disclose AI-generated content, and in October 2026 Etsy banned the sale of AI-generated “functional guides” (recipes, patterns, instructions).
  • Even a digital product, once created, needs updates, customer support, promotion and administration.
  • What can become partly passive is the income from a good product, in a well-chosen niche, supported by a promotion channel that works. Getting there takes active work at the start.

In this book, then, we’ll talk about scalable income (a product you can sell many times without remaking it) and service income (paid work for a client), with realistic expectations for both.

  • It doesn’t think like a person: it produces plausible responses based on what it has learned.
  • It has reduced the production cost of content; value is shifting to choice, verification, relationships and responsibility.
  • “Passive income” without work is a myth: scalable income and service income both exist, and both require upfront effort.

Chapter 2

How Generative AI Works

You don’t need to become an engineer to use AI well, just as you don’t need to understand an engine to drive a car. But knowing roughly how it works lets you get better results and predict when it will make mistakes.

Language models in simple words

Tools like ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude are based on Large Language Models, or LLMs.

A language model has been “trained” by reading an enormous quantity of text: books, web pages, articles, code, documents. During training it learned one thing only, but to an extraordinary degree: predicting which piece of text is most likely to come next, given everything that came before.

When you type a question, the model doesn’t “look up the answer” in an archive. It builds the answer one small piece at a time, each time choosing the most suitable piece based on context: your question, the instructions you gave, the previous conversation.

What tokens are

Those “small pieces” are called tokens. A token can be a whole word, part of a word or a punctuation mark. In English, a word corresponds on average to a little more than one token. Tokens matter for two practical reasons:

  1. Memory limit. Each model can consider a maximum number of tokens at a time (the so-called context window). Today this window is very large, but in very long conversations the model can “forget” or confuse the initial instructions.
  2. Cost. When you use models through pay-as-you-go services, you pay according to the number of tokens processed.

Why AI seems intelligent

To predict the next text well, the model had to learn a huge number of patterns: grammar, style, the most common facts, the structure of reasoning, how to write a complaint letter or a marketing plan. That’s why it can perform very different tasks without having been specifically programmed for each one.

After initial training, models are further refined with the help of people who evaluate their answers, so that they become more useful, clearer and safer. It’s this phase that makes them able to follow instructions and hold a conversation.

Why AI gets things wrong with confidence

Now that you know the mechanism, you also understand its main flaw. The model produces what is plausible, not necessarily what is true. If it lacks reliable information on a topic, it still tends to produce an answer that looks credible. This phenomenon is called hallucination.

Typical examples of hallucinations:

  • quotes attributed to people who never said them;
  • titles of books or articles that don’t exist;
  • precise but invented statistics;
  • laws described approximately or in outdated form;
  • product prices and features that are no longer current.

Warning. Whenever AI-generated content contains a verifiable fact (a number, a date, a name, a law, a price, a quote), check it against a reliable source before using it, especially if the content will be sold or published on a client’s behalf.

Many tools today can search the web and cite sources. This reduces hallucinations but doesn’t eliminate them: sources must also be opened and read, because the model can summarize them badly.

The model’s “knowledge date”

Every model has a knowledge cutoff: it knows the world well up to a certain point and little or nothing of what happened afterwards, unless it uses web search. If you ask for a service’s current prices without enabling search, you might get information that’s months out of date.

How image, audio and video generators work

The principle is similar, even though the technique is different.

  • Images. Image generators have been trained on huge numbers of “image + description” pairs. When you write a description, the system starts from an image made of random “noise” and gradually transforms it into an image consistent with the text. That’s why two identical requests produce different images.
  • Voice and audio. Voice models have learned the characteristics of the human voice (rhythm, intonation, timbre) and can read a text with very natural synthetic voices, or clone a voice from a sample, which requires the consent of the person whose voice it is.
  • Video. Video generators produce short sequences from text or an image. In 2026 quality has improved a lot, but videos remain short (often a few seconds per generation), cost credits and require several attempts to get a usable result.

What this means for you in practice

How it works Practical consequence What to do
The model predicts the most plausible text Generic answers if the question is generic Give context, examples and a precise objective
It doesn’t distinguish true from plausible Possible convincing errors Check facts, numbers and sources
It has a knowledge cutoff Information sometimes outdated Use web search and check dates
Each generation is different Variable results Generate several variations and choose
It has a context window In long chats it “forgets” Summarize and start a new chat

What we learned

  • Language models build answers one token at a time, predicting the most likely continuation.
  • They seem intelligent because they have learned the patterns of language and reasoning.
  • They produce what is plausible, not necessarily true: “hallucinations” are a structural risk.
  • Image, voice and video generators follow similar logic and always produce different results.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Treating AI as an infallible search engine.
  • Publishing numbers, quotes or laws without checking them.
  • Continuing the same conversation endlessly when the model starts getting confused.

Practical exercise

Open an AI chat tool on its free plan and ask: “List three books published on the topic of time management, with author and year.” Then check each title in an online bookstore or library catalog. Note how many results are correct, how many have wrong details, and how many, if any, don’t exist.

Chapter 3

ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and the Other Main Tools

There are hundreds of AI tools, but to start you only need a few. In this chapter you’ll get to know general-purpose AI assistants, the tools you’ll use every day to write, reason, research and plan. Specialized tools (images, video, voice, websites, automation) are covered in Part 3.

What an AI assistant is

An AI assistant (or chatbot) is an application that lets you have a conversation with a language model. You type a request in a text box, get an answer, and can continue the conversation by asking for changes, more detail or new versions. The leading assistants today also let you:

  • upload documents (PDFs, spreadsheets, presentations) and have them analyzed;
  • upload images and ask for them to be described or for their text to be extracted;
  • search the web for up-to-date information;
  • generate images (some directly, others through connected tools);
  • use your voice to speak and listen;
  • create projects or workspaces with saved instructions and documents.

The three main assistants

ChatGPT (OpenAI)

This is the best-known assistant and the one through which many people discovered generative AI. It’s versatile, has a simple interface, and integrates image generation, web search, file analysis and voice mode.

  • Strengths: great versatility, a large ecosystem, built-in image generation, plenty of tutorials available.
  • Weaknesses: usage limits on the free plan are reached quickly with intensive work; the default writing style tends to be recognizable and needs guiding.
  • Plans (checked November 2026, US prices): free; Go $8/month (localized pricing in some countries); Plus $20/month; Pro from $100/month, with higher tiers.

Gemini (Google)

This is Google’s assistant, integrated with the Google ecosystem (Gmail, Docs, Drive, YouTube). It’s especially convenient if you already work with Google tools.

  • Strengths: integration with Google Workspace, good handling of long documents and multimedia content, a subscription that includes cloud storage.
  • Weaknesses: some features vary from country to country; the plan lineup has changed several times.
  • Plans (checked November 2026): free; Google AI Plus (listed at €4.99/month in some European countries); Google AI Pro ($19.99/month in the US; European prices vary by country); Ultra for heavy use.

Claude (Anthropic)

This is the assistant developed by Anthropic, valued in particular for the quality of its writing, its ability to work on long documents and to follow detailed instructions.

  • Strengths: natural, polished writing, analysis of long documents, close adherence to instructions, projects with reference documents.
  • Weaknesses: usage limits on the free plan can be tight for long tasks.
  • Plans (checked November 2026, US prices): free; Pro $20/month with monthly billing; Max $100/month or $200/month for very heavy use.

Tip. Don’t subscribe to everything right away. For two weeks, use the free versions of at least two assistants for the same tasks. Then subscribe to just one, the one that gave you the best results for the kind of work you want to do. One subscription used well is worth more than three used halfway.

Getting started, step by step

  1. Go to the official website of the tool you’ve chosen (type the address yourself; don’t click sponsored ads: there are clone sites that imitate famous tools).
  2. Create an account with your email or a Google/Apple/Microsoft account.
  3. Open the settings and look for the section on data and privacy. Consider turning off the use of your conversations to train models, if the option is available.
  4. Look for the custom instructions section (or “personalization,” “memory”): here you can write who you are and how you prefer answers. Example: “I’m a beginner. Answer in English, simply, with numbered steps when you explain a procedure.”
  5. Make your first request: “Explain in five points what you can do to help me organize my work week.”

Warning. Chapter 4

How to Write Effective Prompts

A prompt is the instruction you give to AI. It’s the most important skill in this book: the same tool can produce a bland text or a professional one depending on how you ask.

Why generic prompts give generic results

Remember how models work? They produce the most plausible continuation given the context. If the context is thin (“write an Instagram post for a shop”), the most plausible answer is an average post, just like a thousand others. If the context is rich (who the shop is, who it’s for, what the post should achieve, what tone it uses, which examples it likes), the answer becomes specific and useful.

The RCOFE method: five elements of a good prompt

So you don’t forget anything, use this five-part framework. It isn’t a magic formula: it’s a checklist.

Element Question it answers Example
Role Who should the AI “be”? “Act as an experienced copywriter for small local businesses.”
Context What’s the situation? “I own an artisan bakery in Bergamo; my customers are local families and nearby offices.”
Objective What do I want to achieve? “I want more orders for custom birthday cakes.”
Format What should the result look like? “Three Instagram posts, maximum 80 words each, ending with a question.”
Examples & limits What to imitate, what to avoid? “Warm tone, no excessive emojis, don’t mention prices. Here’s a post I like: …”

From weak prompt to effective prompt

Weak prompt:

Write a post for my bakery.

Effective prompt:

Act as an experienced copywriter specializing in communication
for small local businesses.

Context: I own an artisan bakery in Bergamo. My customers are mostly
families from the neighborhood and employees from nearby offices.
My specialty is custom cakes made with seasonal ingredients.

Objective: increase requests for custom birthday cakes,
to be ordered at least 3 days in advance.

Format: write 3 Instagram posts, maximum 80 words each.
Each post must open with an attention-grabbing sentence
and end with an invitation to send us a direct message.

Limits: warm, friendly tone, maximum 2 emojis per post,
don't mention prices. Avoid clichés like "uncompromising quality."

The difference in the result is huge, and the extra time to write the second prompt is about two minutes.

Six techniques that make a difference

1. Give concrete examples

If you have a text you like, paste it in and write: “Use this as a reference for tone and length; don’t copy its content.” Examples communicate style better than any adjective.

2. Ask before doing

For important work, add at the end of your prompt: “Before you start, ask me up to 5 questions to get any information you’re missing.” The AI will help you clarify what you hadn’t specified.

3. Break big tasks into steps

Don’t ask “write a 50-page ebook.” First ask for the table of contents, then correct it, then ask for one chapter at a time, each time providing the approved table of contents and summaries of the previous chapters.

4. Ask for variations, then choose

“Suggest 10 different titles, numbered, in different styles: some direct, some intriguing, some with a number.” Then: “Develop numbers 3 and 7.” Choosing is faster than describing.

5. Have the result critiqued

“Now reread the text as a strict editor. List the 5 weakest points and suggest an improved version.” This technique, called self-critique, greatly improves quality.

6. Say what you don’t want

Models tend to use recurring formulas. Listing expressions to avoid (“in today’s fast-paced world,” “discover,” “revolutionary,” “in conclusion”) makes the text more natural.

Iterative prompting: you work in conversation

The first result is rarely the final one. The typical flow is:

  1. Initial prompt, complete (using the RCOFE framework).
  2. Evaluation: what works? What doesn’t?
  3. Targeted correction: “The second paragraph is too formal. Rewrite it as if you were talking to a friend; keep the information.”
  4. Repeat until you get the result.
  5. Final human review: read everything, check the facts, fix the details by hand.

Tip. Create a document called “My prompts” and save the ones that work. In a few weeks you’ll have a personal library that saves you many hours. Part 6 of this book gives you a starting base of more than fifty prompts.

Prompts for images: a different language

For image generators the framework changes. They work best with concrete visual descriptions organized like this:

  1. Subject: what’s in the image.
  2. Setting: where it is.
  3. Style: photography, illustration, watercolor, 3D, minimalist…
  4. Light and color: soft morning light, pastel palette, strong contrast…
  5. Framing: close-up, overhead, wide angle…
  6. Format: square, vertical 4:5, horizontal 16:9.

Example:

Overhead photograph of a light wooden desk with an open notebook,
a cup of coffee and a small plant. Soft natural morning light from
the left, neutral beige and sage green palette, minimalist style,
lots of empty space on the right for adding text.
Vertical 4:5 format.

What we learned

  • A prompt rich in context produces specific results; a vague prompt produces average results.
  • The RCOFE method (Role, Context, Objective, Format, Examples & limits) is an effective checklist.
  • Examples, preliminary questions, breaking work into steps, variations and self-critique greatly improve results.
  • Images require visual descriptions: subject, setting, style, light, framing, format.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Settling for the first result.
  • Asking for huge jobs in a single prompt.
  • Not specifying tone, length and audience.
  • Publishing the text without a human review.

Practical exercise

Choose a real business you know (a shop in your neighborhood, a professional, your own company). Write a weak prompt and an RCOFE prompt to get a 100-word description for its Google profile. Run both and compare the results. Then apply the self-critique technique to the better result.

Many people give up before they start because they think AI is “for computer people.” In reality, today’s tools are designed to be used by writing or speaking, just as you would with a collaborator. Ten everyday uses to build confidence

The best way to learn is to use AI for real problems, small and frequent. Here are ten exercises to do over a week.

  1. Rewrite a difficult email. “Rewrite this email so it’s polite but firm: [text].”
  2. Summarize a long document. Upload a PDF and ask: “Summarize it in 10 points, then list the deadlines and figures it contains.”
  3. Make a plan. “Help me organize a house move over 4 weeks. Create a table with week, task and person responsible.”
  4. Explain a topic. “Explain Italy’s flat-rate tax regime (regime forfettario) as if I were 15, then tell me what questions to ask an accountant.”
  5. Brainstorm. “Give me 20 birthday gift ideas for someone who loves gardening, with a budget of €40.”
  6. Edit a text. “Correct the grammar and punctuation without changing the style. List the corrections you made.”
  7. Translate and adapt. “Translate this ad into English, adapting it for a British audience.”
  8. Analyze a spreadsheet. Upload a file of expenses and ask: “What are the three highest spending categories? Create a chart.”
  9. Prepare for an interview or negotiation. “Play a demanding client who wants a discount. I’m the supplier. Let’s begin.”
  10. Create a simple image. “Create a square image for an invitation to a dinner with friends, illustrated style, warm colors.”

Using your voice

Almost every assistant has a voice mode. It’s very useful if you type slowly or want to “think out loud”: you can describe an idea while walking and then ask the assistant to turn it into an organized text. You can also dictate the context of a long prompt and then correct it in writing.

Working with “projects” and saved instructions

The leading assistants let you create projects (or spaces, or custom assistants) where you save instructions and reference documents that apply to every conversation in that project. It’s the most useful feature when you work continuously for a client or on a product.

Example of project instructions for a client:

You are the communications assistant for Aurora Physical Therapy
(a fictional client). Audience: adults aged 35-65 with back and
neck pain, amateur athletes.
Tone: professional, reassuring, never alarmist.
Rules: don't diagnose, don't promise recovery, always invite people
to an in-clinic assessment. Use "you." No emojis in long texts.
Attached documents: service price list, FAQ, 5 posts the client
approved in the past (use them as a style reference).

How much time you’ll really save

Go back to the list of three activities you wrote in Chapter 1. For each one, try doing it with AI and measure the time, including review. Many people discover that:

  • for first drafts, the savings are large;
  • for texts that require precise information, the savings are smaller, because checking takes time;
  • for high-quality creative work, AI speeds up the early stage, but the final polish remains human.

This personal measurement is more reliable than any general statistic: it tells you where AI really makes you more productive.

What we learned

  • To use AI, you only need to be able to write and use a browser.
  • A free minimum kit is enough to get started.
  • A tidy folder structure is the first professional step.
  • Small, real everyday uses are the fastest way to learn.
  • “Projects” with saved instructions are ideal for recurring clients and products.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting to “understand everything” before you start.
  • Working without organization and losing files and prompts.
  • Measuring time saved without including review.

Chapter 6

Opportunities, Limits, Mistakes and Risks to Know

Before moving on to concrete ways to make money, you need an honest picture of what AI can and can’t do, of beginners’ most common mistakes and of the risks to manage. This chapter is an operational overview; Part 8 goes deeper into legal and security issues.

The real opportunities

Generative AI creates opportunities in four directions:

  1. Doing what you already know faster. If you’re good at writing, teaching, organizing or selling, AI multiplies your productivity.
  2. Doing things you couldn’t afford before. Graphics, short videos, translations, simple websites: today a beginner can reach a decent level with inexpensive tools.
  3. Helping others use AI. Many small businesses know they should use it but don’t have time to learn. This creates demand for services.
  4. Creating digital products. Guides, templates, courses and resources can be produced at low cost and sold many times, if they solve a real problem.

The limits to accept

Limit What it means How to manage it
Errors and hallucinations Invented facts presented with confidence Always check facts
Genericness “Average” texts, similar to many others Rich context, examples, revision in your own voice
No lived experience AI hasn’t tried what it talks about Add your own experience, tests and real cases
Inconsistency Different results with every attempt Saved instructions, examples, final check
Limits with hands, text and details in images Frequent visual defects Check at full zoom, retouch
Platform dependence Prices and rules change Diversify tools and channels

Beginners’ most common mistakes

  1. Flooding the market with generic products. Publishing dozens of ebooks or prints without care leads to zero sales, negative reviews and sometimes account closure.
  2. Copying the competition. Using AI to remake another seller’s product is unfair, often illegal, and doesn’t set you apart anyway.
  3. Underestimating promotion. Creating the product is 30% of the work; getting it known is the rest.
  4. Prices that are too low. Cutting prices to sell attracts the most difficult clients and makes the business unsustainable.
  5. Ignoring platform rules. Every platform has rules on AI content, intellectual property and transparency: breaking them can cost you your account.
  6. Forgetting taxes and social contributions. Even small earnings can have tax consequences.
  7. Chasing every new tool. Switching tools every week stops you from getting good at any one of them.

The main risks in brief

  • Legal: copyright, using other people’s trademarks, images of real people without consent, misleading advertising.
  • Privacy: entering clients’ personal data into AI tools without a legal basis and adequate safeguards.
  • Transparency: in the European Union, since 2 August 2026 the transparency obligations of Article 50 of the EU Artificial Intelligence Regulation (AI Act) apply, covering, among other things, the labeling of deepfakes and certain generated content. We discuss this in Part 8.
  • Reputation: an error published on a client’s behalf damages both you and them.
  • Financial: useless subscriptions, expensive courses sold with false promises, scams.

Warning. Be wary of anyone who sells you a “system” for making money with AI while showing only screenshots of income. A real business is judged by its products, clients, verifiable reviews and transparency about costs, not by screenshots of an account.

Chapter 7

Creating and Selling Ebooks

What it is and how it works

An ebook is a digital book, usually in PDF or EPUB format, that the customer downloads and reads on a computer, tablet, phone or e-reader. In this chapter we’re talking about ebooks sold directly by you: on your website, through platforms such as Gumroad, Payhip or Lemon Squeezy, or through social media. Publishing on Amazon and selling on Etsy each have their own chapter, because they follow specific rules.

It works simply: you create the file once, upload it to a sales platform, and every time someone pays they automatically receive the download link. AI helps you with research, structure, drafting, revision, the cover and promotion. It does not replace your expertise on the subject: an ebook is valuable if it contains useful knowledge, organized better than what the reader could find for free.

Why someone would pay

Almost everything can be found free on the internet. People pay for an ebook when it offers at least one of these benefits:

  • Time saved: it gathers into an orderly path what is scattered elsewhere.
  • Specificity: it answers a precise problem for a precise audience (“weekly menus for families with a celiac child,” not “healthy recipes”).
  • Trust: it’s written by someone with demonstrable experience in the subject.
  • Applicability: it contains frameworks, checklists, examples and exercises to use right away.

The best-selling ebooks aren’t the ones “about a topic,” but the ones that take the reader from situation A to situation B.

Step-by-step procedure to get started

  1. Choose a topic in which you have experience, or can verifiably acquire it. List 10 things you do better than average or problems you’ve solved for yourself or others.
  2. Define the reader. Write one sentence: “This ebook is for [who] who wants [result] but is stuck because of [problem].”
  3. Check demand. Search for the topic on Amazon, in Facebook groups, on Reddit, in YouTube comments. Look for recurring questions and complaints about existing books (“too theoretical,” “missing an example of…”).
  4. Create the table of contents with AI. Use a prompt with the reader, the goal and the problems you’ve collected. Ask for 3 alternative outlines, choose the best and correct it by hand.
  5. Write one chapter at a time. Give the AI the table of contents, the chapter to write, your notes and experiences. Ask for a draft, then rewrite it in your own voice, adding your own examples.
  6. Check the facts. Every number, regulation, health or legal tip must be checked against reliable sources.
  7. Add practical tools. Checklists, tables, exercises, templates: these are what readers perceive as value.
  8. Get it read. At least two people from your target audience. Ask: “What didn’t you understand? What did you skip? What would you use tomorrow?”
  9. Lay it out and create the cover. A title that’s readable even as a thumbnail, a subtitle that states the result.
  10. Prepare the sales page. Title, who it’s for, what it contains, what it doesn’t, table of contents, free excerpt, author, price, guarantee.
  11. Upload it to a sales platform and make a test purchase.

How to find your first customers

  • Your network. Announce the project while you’re writing it: people who follow the process are more likely to buy.
  • Waiting list. Create a free page (for example with your email marketing tool) where people leave their email to be notified at launch.
  • Valuable free content. Publish excerpts, checklists and tips from the ebook on social media or in groups where your readers are, following the groups’ rules.
  • Collaborations. Offer professionals with a related audience (not competitors) an exchange: you offer free content to their followers, they mention the ebook.

How to set prices

The price of an ebook sold directly depends on how specific it is and what result it promises:

Type of ebook Common price range Notes
Short generic guide (20–40 pages) €5–12 Lots of free competition
Specific guide with practical tools €15–29 The value is in the specificity
Professional guide with templates and resources €29–59 For people who earn or save money with it

These ranges come from observing the market and are indicative. A simple method: look at 10 similar products, discard the most expensive and the cheapest, and position yourself based on how much more specific and complete yours is.

How to make your first sale

  1. Before launch, personally contact 20 people who match your ideal reader (not just friends) and offer the ebook at a launch price in exchange for honest feedback.
  2. On launch day, email your waiting list with an offer valid for 72 hours.
  3. Publish content that shows a real page of the ebook and the problem it solves.
  4. Answer every question publicly: questions are your best market research.

  3. Research: in parenting groups she finds recurring questions about unexpected expenses, utility bills and grocery spending.
  4. Product: a 45-page ebook + a template spreadsheet + 4 monthly checklists.

Chapter 8

Creating Digital Products and Templates

What it is and how it works

Digital products are files the customer downloads or accesses online and uses to get a task done. This category includes: Canva templates, ready-made spreadsheets (budgets, client management, planning), printable planners, document templates (contracts to be checked by a lawyer, quotes, procedures), social media kits, presentations, Notion templates, professional checklists.

The difference from an ebook is that the customer isn’t buying information to read but a tool to use. That’s an advantage: the value is immediate and easy to show in a preview.

Why someone would pay

A good template saves hours of work and reduces errors. A hairdresser who buys 30 Canva post templates isn’t paying for the graphics as such: they’re paying not to have to design from scratch every week. A freelancer who buys a spreadsheet for quotes and invoices is paying for order and peace of mind.

People pay when the product is:

  • specific to a trade or situation (templates for physical therapists, not “social media templates”);
  • ready to use, with clear instructions;
  • customizable without technical skills;
  • well presented, with previews that show the finished result.

Chapter 9

Selling on Etsy

What it is and how it works

Etsy is an international marketplace for handmade products, vintage items, craft supplies and digital products (instant downloads). For many beginners it’s the first place to sell printables, planners, templates, invitations, graphics and other files, because it already brings an audience of buyers actively looking for products.

It works like this: you open a shop, create listings (title, photos, description, tags, price, file), and Etsy shows your products in its search results. When a customer buys a digital product, they receive the file automatically.

Etsy’s rules on AI (updated November 2026)

This is the first thing to understand, because it has changed recently:

  • Etsy’s Creativity Standards (in force since 2024) only allow you to sell items you have made, designed, handpicked or sourced yourself. Digital products you design, even with the help of AI, fall under the “Designed by” category.
  • You must disclose when a product was created with AI, and you can’t present it as handmade.
  • With the update of 22 October 2026, Etsy banned AI-generated “functional guides”: patterns (for example knitting or sewing), recipes and instructions. Disclosing the use of AI isn’t enough to make them allowed. Anyone selling guides of this kind must be able to show the result with a real item made by following the instructions.
  • Listings created by bots without human involvement are prohibited.
  • Digital downloads must include a preview or excerpt of the content.

Warning. News about these rules comes from Etsy’s announcement and press coverage (for example CNBC, 22 October 2026). Before opening your shop, read the Creativity Standards and the House Rules directly on etsy.com: they’re the only binding source.

Practical consequence: on Etsy it makes sense to sell digital products in which your creative and design contribution is clear (design, selection, composition, tested functionality), and not informational content or instructions generated by AI.

Why someone would pay

People who buy on Etsy are looking for ready-made, beautiful, personalized solutions: a coordinated wedding invitation, a study planner, a pantry label kit, a menu template for a restaurant. They pay for design, consistency, practicality and time saved.

Step-by-step procedure to get started

  1. Study the niche. Type words like “planner,” “template,” “printable” into Etsy’s search and note the automatic suggestions: they’re real user searches.
  2. Analyze 20 products that sell in the chosen niche: price, number of reviews, photo style, what customers complain about in negative reviews.
  3. Find a different angle. A more specific audience (primary school teachers, wedding planners, people with a balcony vegetable garden), a different style, an extra feature.
  4. Create a consistent collection of 10–15 products in the same style, rather than random products.
  5. Create the shop: name, logo, banner, information, policies (for digital downloads, refunds are generally not offered except for defective files: state this clearly).
  6. Prepare the listings:
    • a title with the main keywords, written for people and not just for the algorithm;
    • 13 tags (the maximum allowed) with different keywords;
    • 5–10 images: a first image that’s clear even as a thumbnail, then details, use, format, instructions;
    • a description: what the buyer receives, formats, sizes, how to use it, what’s not included, a disclosure of AI use where relevant.
  7. Upload the files and make a test purchase (or check the download with the preview).

How to find your first customers

  • Etsy’s internal search: this is the main channel. Precise titles and tags matter more than anything.
  • Pinterest: create vertical pins for each product, linked to the listing.
  • Personal network and social media: the first reviews are the hardest; ask only people who have actually bought and used the product, without offering compensation (incentivized reviews break the rules).
  • Etsy Ads: consider them only after optimizing your listings, starting with very small budgets and measuring results.

Always calculate the net per sale.

Publishing Books on Amazon KDP

What it is and how it works

Kindle Direct Publishing (KDP) is Amazon’s self-publishing platform. It lets you publish Kindle ebooks, paperbacks and, in some markets, hardcovers for free. Amazon prints the book only when someone orders it (print on demand), ships it and pays you a royalty, a percentage of the price.

You pay nothing to publish: Amazon keeps its share of every copy sold. This makes KDP accessible, but also extremely crowded: a huge number of new titles are published every day, many of them mass-produced and of low quality.

KDP’s rules on AI

When you publish, KDP asks you to state whether the book contains AI-generated content:

  • AI-generated content (text, images or translations created by an AI tool) must be disclosed, even if you’ve edited it substantially.
  • AI-assisted content (you wrote it and used AI to edit, improve or brainstorm) does not need to be disclosed.

KDP also limits the number of titles that can be published in a day and removes content that offers a poor reader experience (for example books that summarize or copy other books, content without value, misleading titles). The precise rules are in the “Content Guidelines” section of the KDP Help pages.

Why someone would pay

On Amazon, people look for books to solve problems, learn, relax or give as gifts. They buy when the title, cover, reviews and preview (“Look inside”) convince them. The categories that work best for people starting without an audience are niche ones with readers who actively search: specific practical guides, workbooks, activity books with curated content, genre fiction with strong readerships (with real writing work).

Creating Images and Graphics with AI

What it is and how it works

Image generation tools create illustrations, realistic photographs, backgrounds, textures, icons and concepts from a text description or a reference image. AI-powered editors also let you remove backgrounds, extend an image, replace an object, upscale resolution or correct lighting.

There are two ways to earn:

  1. Design services for businesses and professionals: social media graphics, banners, website images, product mockups, presentations, event materials.
  2. Visual products: prints, illustrations for digital products, packs of backgrounds or design elements (pay attention to platform rules and rights, see below).

Copyright on AI images: what to know first

  • In the United States, the Copyright Office reaffirmed in January 2025 that images generated entirely by AI from a simple prompt cannot be protected by copyright; human contributions (selection, arrangement, significant creative changes) can be, assessed case by case.
  • In the European Union and in Italy, copyright requires a work resulting from human creativity; the status of works generated with AI is the subject of debate and legislation. In Italy, the artificial intelligence law approved in 2025 reaffirmed the requirement of human creative input.
  • Practical consequence: you can’t easily stop others from reusing an image generated entirely by AI. The value of your work lies in the overall design, consistency with the brand and the creative changes.
  • Each generator’s terms of use state whether and how you can use images commercially. Some tools require specific plans for companies above certain revenue thresholds (Midjourney, for example, requires the Pro or Mega plan for companies with revenue above $1 million).

Warning. Don’t generate images that imitate brands, logos, protected characters or the recognizable style of living artists for commercial purposes, and don’t create realistic images of real people without their consent. These are the most common causes of legal problems and removal from platforms.

Why someone would pay

A business doesn’t pay for “an image”: it pays for images consistent with its brand, delivered in the right formats, on time, without errors (deformed hands, misspelled text, altered products) and with the assurance that it can use them legally. Someone who knows how to check and refine AI output offers exactly that assurance.

Offering Copywriting and Writing Services

What it is and how it works

Copywriting is writing with a commercial goal: getting people to buy, sign up, get in touch, book. It includes copy for websites, sales pages, emails, ads, product descriptions, social posts, brochures, video scripts. Alongside copywriting there are other writing services: blog articles (with attention to SEO, i.e. search engine optimization), newsletters, corporate copy, revision and editing, translation and localization.

AI has made producing drafts extremely fast. As a result the market has split: generic low-cost copy has largely been replaced by AI, while there’s demand for professionals who can understand the client, find the right message, verify and guarantee quality.

Why someone would pay

A business could use AI by itself. It pays a copywriter because:

  • it doesn’t know what to say (positioning, differences from competitors, customer objections);
  • it doesn’t have time to guide and correct the AI;
  • it wants copy that’s consistent with its voice and accurate in content;
  • it wants someone accountable for the result (conversions, sign-ups).

Copywriting principles to know

  1. Talk about the customer, not the company. “Sleep without back pain” is stronger than “Our mattress has 7 zones.”

  5. Proof. Real reviews, verifiable numbers, guarantees, concrete examples.
  6. Objections. Answer doubts in advance (price, timing, trust).

    • using AI to extract benefits, objections and the customers’ language;

    • a draft with AI, your own rewrite, fact-checking, proofreading.
  3. Create a portfolio of 3–5 pieces: as an exercise you can rewrite pages of existing businesses (without publishing them in their name), showing before and after.

Chapter 20

Choosing a Profitable Idea

An idea is “profitable” when it meets three conditions: someone has a problem, they’re willing to pay to solve it, and you can reach them at a sustainable cost. Enthusiasm isn’t enough, and neither is the trend of the moment.

The three-circle method

Draw three circles:

  1. What I know how to do (skills, work experience, serious hobbies, problems you’ve solved);
  2. What I enjoy doing enough to keep doing it for months;
  3. What people pay for (paid products, services and courses on the topic already exist).

The best ideas sit where the three overlap. If an idea is only in circle 3, you’ll get tired or won’t be credible. If it’s only in circles 1 and 2, you risk creating a hobby, not a business.

Generating ideas with AI (the right way)

Don’t ask AI “give me a business idea.” Ask it to help you explore your skills:

Here's my background: [work experience, skills, hobbies,
problems I've solved]. Generate 20 ideas for digital products or
services I could offer using AI as a tool. For each one, state:
customer, problem solved, format (service or product), why my
experience is an advantage. Exclude ideas anyone could replicate
in a day without any skills.

Evaluating ideas: the scoring matrix

Criterion Question Score 1–5
Problem Is the problem frequent and annoying?
Payment Are people already paying to solve it?
Access Do I know where to find these people?
Advantage Do I have experience or a distinctive point of view?
Feasibility Can I create a first version in 30 days?
Interest Would I enjoy working on it for at least a year?

Discard ideas with a total under 20 points. Dig deeper into the top two.

Anna’s case

Anna lists: 12 years in a purchasing office, negotiating with suppliers, managing complex spreadsheets, a passion for organization. Among the ideas generated, the highest-scoring are: (a) a template kit for purchasing management in small businesses; (b) a workshop on using AI to prepare negotiations with suppliers. She chooses to explore the first.

What we learned

  • A profitable idea combines a real problem, willingness to pay and the ability to reach customers.
  • The three-circle method helps you start from your own skills.
  • AI is useful for exploring ideas based on your profile, not for inventing them out of nothing.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing the trendiest idea instead of the one that suits you best.
  • Evaluating ideas without written criteria.

Practical exercise

Chapter 21

Finding a Market Niche

A niche is a specific market segment: a group of people with shared characteristics and needs. Narrowing your focus feels scary (“I’ll lose customers!”), but in reality it makes everything easier: you know what to write, where to find customers and how to stand out.

From generic to specific

Too generic More specific Niche
Templates for businesses Purchasing management templates Purchasing management templates for restaurants with 1–3 locations
Cooking courses Quick cooking 20-minute lunches for shift workers

How to check that a niche exists

Chapter 23

Identifying Customer Needs

Online research and AI give you hypotheses. Only real people give you confirmation. This chapter teaches you how to talk to potential customers without “selling,” to understand what they really need.

Customer interviews

Contact 5–10 people in your niche and ask for 20 minutes for a chat (offer to share the results or a small gift). Golden rules:

  1. Ask about the past, not the future. “Tell me about the last time you had this problem” is reliable. “Would you buy a product that…?” isn’t: people say yes to be polite.
  2. Listen more than you talk. Don’t present your idea until the end.
  3. Ask what they’ve already tried and how much they spent.
  4. Write down their exact words.

Useful questions

  • What’s the most annoying part of [activity]?
  • How do you handle it today?
  • What have you tried to improve it? How did it go?
  • How much time or money does this problem cost you?
  • If you had a magic wand, what would you change?

Turning interviews into needs

After the interviews, load your notes into an AI assistant (removing names and personal data) and ask for a summary:

Here are the anonymized notes from 8 interviews with potential customers.
1. List the problems mentioned and how many people mentioned each.
2. State which solutions they've already tried and why they didn't work.
3. Extract the most significant sentences (verbatim).
4. Suggest a one-sentence description of the main need.

Anna’s case

Anna interviews 8 restaurant owners. She discovers that the main problem isn’t inventory as such, but orders to suppliers: things forgotten, double orders, nobody knowing who ordered what. Several interviewees say the same thing: “I do it late at night, tired, on my phone.” The real need: a simple ordering system, shared with staff, usable from a phone in 5 minutes.

What we learned

  • Only interviews with real customers confirm needs.
  • You ask about the past, not about future intentions.
  • AI helps summarize interviews, after anonymization.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Presenting your idea and asking “do you like it?”
  • Loading notes containing personal data into AI.

Practical exercise

Chapter 24

Creating a Product That Solves a Real Problem

Now that you know the need, create the minimum version of the solution: the simplest product that solves the main problem and that you can put in front of customers quickly. It’s called an MVP, a Minimum Viable Product.

The rules of the minimum version

  1. It solves one problem, the main one.
  2. It takes days or a few weeks to create, not months.
  3. It’s good enough to be paid for, even if it isn’t perfect.
  4. It improves with feedback from the first customers.

The promise formula

Write your product’s promise like this:

[Product] helps [customer] to [result] without [main obstacle].

Example: “The Easy Orders kit helps small restaurants manage supplier orders in 5 minutes a day from their phone, without complicated software.”

Using AI to build the product

  • Design: “Design the structure of [product] to solve [need]. List the components, content and format.”
  • First draft of the content: copy, instructions, examples with hypothetical data.
  • Critical review: “Act as a skeptical [typical customer]. Which parts wouldn’t you use? What don’t you understand?”
  • Testing: have 3 real people try it and watch where they get stuck.

Anna’s case

In two weeks Anna creates a kit consisting of: a shared spreadsheet (Google Sheets) usable from a phone with a list of suppliers, products, a “to order” status field and an order history; a 10-page PDF guide; an 8-minute video. She has 3 of the restaurant owners she interviewed try it. Two use it for a week; one gives up because of “too many columns.” Anna simplifies it.

What we learned

  • The minimum version solves one problem and is ready quickly.
  • A product’s promise is written with a simple formula.
  • AI speeds up design, drafts and critical review; tests with real people are irreplaceable.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing perfection before showing the product.
  • Adding features nobody asked for.

Practical exercise

Chapter 25

Setting the Price

Price is one of the most important and most neglected decisions. A price that’s too low doesn’t cover costs, attracts unmotivated customers and signals poor quality. A well-thought-out price makes the business sustainable.

Three ways to think about price

Method Question Use
Cost-based How much does it cost me to produce and sell? Sets the minimum
Competition-based How much do the alternatives cost? Sets the benchmark
Value-based How much is the result worth to the customer? Sets the maximum possible

The right price sits between the minimum (costs) and the maximum (value), positioned against competitors according to how much more specific, complete or convenient you are.

Calculating the minimum price of a service

  1. Decide how much you want to earn per month before taxes (an assumption).
  2. Estimate your realistic billable hours (not all hours worked are billable: there’s finding clients, admin, training).
  3. Add fixed costs (tools, accountant, insurance).
  4. Account for taxes and social contributions.

Hypothetical example — minimum rate. Goal: €1,500 net per month. Let’s assume taxes and social contributions take about 30% of revenue net of costs (a purely illustrative percentage: yours depends on your tax regime). You’d then need about €2,150 of margin, plus €150 of fixed costs = €2,300 of monthly revenue. With 60 billable hours a month, the minimum rate is about €38 an hour. If that seems high, remember that below this figure the business won’t reach your goal.

Useful pricing techniques

  • Three tiers (basic, standard, complete): many people choose the middle one.
  • A time-limited launch price, stated honestly.
  • Packages instead of hourly rates for services.
  • Subscriptions when the value is ongoing.

Warning. In Italy and the EU, rules on unfair commercial practices prohibit fake discounts (“crossed-out” prices that were never charged) and false deadlines. When announcing a price reduction, in many cases you must state the lowest price charged in the previous 30 days. Always be transparent.

Anna’s case

Costs: tools and platform. Competition: software at €30–80 a month, complex; generic templates at €10–20. Value: a forgotten order can cost a restaurant a ruined menu or a more expensive emergency purchase. Anna chooses: kit at €49; kit + a 45-minute setup session at €129; free updates for a year.

What we learned

  • Price is reasoned through costs (minimum), competition (benchmark) and value (maximum).
  • For services, you calculate a sustainable minimum rate.
  • Tiers, packages and subscriptions help customers choose.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Setting the price only by copying the cheapest competitor.
  • Fake discounts or false urgency.

Practical exercise

Calculate your minimum rate (services) or your net per sale (products). Then define three offer tiers with prices and contents.

Creating your identity with AI

  1. Name: ask for 30 suggestions with criteria (short, evocative, no words from well-known brands). Then check: domain, social media, trademark databases (in Italy the UIBM; in the EU the EUIPO; free search tools such as TMview).

Opening a Digital Store

A digital store is where customers pay and receive what they’ve bought. It can be a marketplace (Etsy, Amazon), a creator platform (Gumroad, Payhip, Stan Store), a website with sales features (Shopify, WooCommerce) or, for services, a simple page with a contact form and payment by link.

How to choose

Need Suitable solution
I want an audience that’s already there Marketplace (Etsy, Amazon KDP)
I sell digital products to my own audience Gumroad, Payhip, Stan Store
I want maximum control and my own brand A website with e-commerce
I sell services Service page + booking + invoice and payment

Essential elements of a sales page

  1. A headline with the main result.
  2. Who it’s for (and who it isn’t for).
  3. What it contains, as a list.
  4. Real previews of the product.
  5. Who you are and why you can help.
  6. Price and what’s included.
  7. Frequently asked questions (formats, refunds, support).
  8. Legal information: terms of sale, privacy, seller details.

Mistakes to avoid

Chapter 29

Getting Your First Customers

The first customers are the hardest, because you don’t yet have reviews, cases or word of mouth. That’s why, in the early stages, direct contact works better than any advertising.

The most effective strategies at the start

  1. Personal and professional network. Write to 30 people you know explaining what you do and asking not that they buy, but whether they know someone it might help.
  2. Targeted direct outreach. Personalized messages to potential customers, with something of concrete value (a specific tip, an example made for them).
  3. Communities. Join the groups where your audience is and answer questions generously.
  4. Workshops and meetings. One hour of real value builds more trust than a hundred posts.
  5. Partners. Professionals serving the same audience with different services.

The first-contact message

Structure:

  1. Personalization (why you’re writing to them specifically);
  2. A useful observation;
  3. A small, concrete proposal;
  4. A simple question.
Hi Marco, I saw your restaurant has just added a lunch menu
(congratulations on the seasonal dishes!).
I work with small restaurants on organizing supplier orders:
many tell me it's the most tiring part of the evening.
I've put together a free weekly ordering checklist: may I send it
to you? If it's useful, we could talk for 15 minutes.

Warning. When contacting businesses and people, follow the rules on privacy and direct marketing: in Italy and the EU, sending commercial messages by email, SMS or WhatsApp to individuals generally requires consent, and B2B outreach should also be respectful, personalized and not mass-sent. Don’t buy contact lists.

Turning first customers into proof

  • Ask for structured feedback after use.
  • Ask for consent to publish a testimonial (real, with name or initials, as the customer prefers).
  • Document a case: starting situation, what you did, measured result.

What we learned

  • At the start, direct contact works better than advertising.
  • A good message is personalized, useful, small and clear.
  • First customers become proof: feedback, real testimonials, measured cases.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Generic messages copied and pasted to hundreds of people.
  • Buying contact lists.
  • Inventing or “embellishing” testimonials.

Chapter 30

Building Customer Loyalty

Acquiring a new customer costs time and money; a satisfied customer who comes back or recommends you costs much less. Loyalty is what turns occasional sales into a stable business.

The levers of loyalty

  1. An excellent first experience: on-time delivery, clear instructions, a welcome message.
  2. Fast, courteous support.
  3. Product updates and improvements.
  4. Regular communication (a useful newsletter, not just promotions).
  5. Dedicated offers for existing customers.
  6. Listening: periodically ask what to improve and show what you’ve changed thanks to their suggestions.

The post-purchase journey

Moment Action
Immediately Welcome email with instructions and a support contact
After 3 days “Have you run into any difficulties?” with a practical tip
After 2 weeks Request for feedback and, if satisfied, a review
After 1–2 months Offer of a complementary product or service
Periodically Updates, useful content

This journey can be automated with email marketing tools. AI helps you write the emails, but the tone must remain yours.

Handling complaints

A complaint handled well can create a more loyal customer than before:

  1. thank them for raising it;
  2. acknowledge the problem without making excuses;
  3. offer a concrete, fast solution;
  4. check that the problem is solved;
  5. improve the product so it doesn’t happen again.

Anna’s case

Anna creates a sequence of 4 automated emails, a WhatsApp group for customers (with clear rules and consent) where she shares kit updates, and an open quarterly video call. After 3 months, half the customers of the basic kit buy the setup session.

What we learned

  • Keeping customers costs less than acquiring new ones.
  • First experience, support and regular communication are the main levers.
  • The post-purchase journey can be automated.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Disappearing after the sale.
  • Sending only promotional emails.
  • Ignoring or disputing complaints.

Chapter 31

Automating and Growing the Business

When the business works, the risk is that your time becomes the limit. Automating means letting tools perform repetitive tasks; growing means increasing results without proportionally increasing your working hours.

What to automate (and what not to)

To automate To keep human
Delivery of digital products Relationships with important customers
Welcome and follow-up emails Strategic decisions
Collecting and organizing requests Final quality control
Scheduling posts Replies to sensitive complaints
Report drafts Interpreting results
Invoicing and payment reminders Negotiations

An automation example

Contact request → reply in 5 minutes (with Zapier or Make):

  1. A customer fills in the form on the website.
  2. The data goes into a spreadsheet.
  3. AI classifies the request (product, service, support) and writes a draft reply.
  4. You receive a notification with the draft; you check it and send it.

The final human step matters: it prevents errors and maintains quality.

Ways to grow

  1. Raise your price when you have proof and demand.
  2. Complementary products for existing customers.
  3. From service to product: turn what you always do for clients into a kit or a course.
  4. New channels after consolidating the first.
  5. Collaborators for tasks you don’t need to do yourself.
  6. Partnerships with people serving the same audience.

The numbers to keep under control every month

Indicator Why
Revenue and costs To know whether the business is sustainable
Margin per product/service To understand what really pays
New customers and where they came from To know which channel works
Repeat purchase rate To measure loyalty
Hours worked To understand your real hourly earnings

Anna’s case

After six months Anna automates delivery, emails and request collection; she creates a 90-minute recorded course on purchasing management for restaurants, based on the questions she’s received; she partners with a restaurant consultant who offers the kit to their clients. She raises the price of the setup session by 20% thanks to her documented cases.

What we learned

  • Automate repetitive tasks, not relationships and quality control.
  • Grow through price, complementary products, products derived from services, channels, collaborators and partners.
  • A few monthly indicators are enough to guide decisions.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Automating before you have a process that works manually.
  • Growing by adding channels before consolidating the first.
  • Not tracking your real hourly earnings.

Practical exercise

List all the tasks you do in a typical week and mark the repetitive ones. Choose one and design a simple automation with a trigger, actions and a final human check.

Item Choice

what is NOT included, a note on AI use.

The content and checklists were written and verified by the author based on her own experience. Artificial intelligence tools were used to edit the text and for some decorative elements.

Scams, Rights, Privacy and Transparency

Online scams and false income promises

The “make money with AI” topic also attracts scammers. Recognizing them saves you money and time.

Warning signs

Sign Why it’s suspicious
Promises of precise, fast income (“€10,000 a month in 60 days”) Nobody can guarantee results: they depend on too many factors
Income screenshots as the only proof Easy to manipulate; they don’t show costs, refunds, taxes
Artificial urgency (“today only,” “last 3 places” repeated) Designed to stop you thinking
The “secret method” isn’t explained before purchase It often doesn’t exist
The main income comes from recruiting other people A typical structure of pyramid schemes
Requests for payment in cryptocurrency or to anonymous foreign accounts Makes recovery impossible
Software or “bots” that promise to generate income by themselves Often scams, sometimes malware
“AI trading” investments with guaranteed returns In finance, guaranteed returns are a classic sign of fraud; always check authorization with the regulators (in Italy, Consob publishes a list of blocked websites and of authorized intermediaries)

Other common scams

  • Clone sites of famous AI tools, reached through sponsored ads: they steal data and payments.
  • Fake job offers as an “AI trainer” or “content evaluator” that ask for an upfront payment for training or equipment.
  • Fake customers who send a payment larger than what’s owed and ask for the difference back.
  • Phishing of platform accounts (Etsy, Amazon, Meta) with emails imitating official communications.

Warning. Golden rule: legitimate platforms never ask for your password by email or message. Always sign in by typing the official address, turn on two-factor authentication on every account and use a password manager.

How you should behave

All the rules that apply to others apply to you too: don’t promise income to your clients or students, don’t show unverifiable results, don’t create false urgency. In Italy and the EU unfair commercial practices are penalized (in Italy the Competition and Market Authority, AGCM, oversees them).

Copyright and intellectual property

What you can protect

  • Text, images and designs with human creative input are generally protectable by copyright.
  • Content generated entirely by AI from a simple prompt has uncertain or no protection in many jurisdictions. In the United States, the Copyright Office (report of January 2025) reaffirmed that human input is required; human contributions (selection, arrangement, creative modifications) can be protected case by case. In Italy, the 2025 artificial intelligence law confirmed that works are protected if they result from human intellectual effort, even when created with the help of AI.
  • Practical consequence: document your creative process (drafts, notes, edits) and always add a significant personal contribution to the products you sell.

What you can’t use

  • Other people’s registered trademarks and trade names in your products (for example film characters, sports logos, famous brand names).
  • Other people’s protected works as a basis to copy or “rework” with AI.
  • The recognizable style of living artists for commercial products that could be confused with their work: besides the legal risks, it’s ethically wrong.
  • Images, fonts and music without a commercial license.

Tools’ terms of use

Every tool’s terms of use state who holds the rights to the output, whether you can use it commercially and within what limits. Read them before selling anything produced with that tool, and reread them when you change plans.

Platform rules

Platform Key rule on AI (November 2026)
Amazon KDP Mandatory disclosure of AI-generated content (text, images, translations); not required for content that is only AI-assisted
Etsy Disclosure of AI use; ban on AI-generated functional guides (recipes, patterns, instructions) since 22 October 2026; ban on bot-created listings
Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, YouTube Labels for realistic content generated or altered with AI; mandatory disclosure in certain cases

Privacy and data protection

In the European Union, the processing of personal data is governed by the GDPR. Some practical rules for anyone working with AI:

  1. Don’t enter third parties’ personal data (clients, guests, patients, employees) into AI tools on consumer plans. If it’s necessary, use business plans with contractual safeguards, and check where the data is processed and whether it’s used for training.
  2. Always anonymize: remove names, contact details, addresses and identifying details before analyzing texts with AI.
  3. Special categories of data (health, sexual orientation, political and religious opinions, biometric data) require even greater caution: avoid processing them with AI tools unless you have specific advice.
  4. If you process data on a client’s behalf you’re a data processor and a written agreement is required.
  5. Your website and email list require a privacy notice, consent management and, if you use non-essential cookies, a compliant cookie banner.
  6. People’s voice and image are personal data: cloning a voice or creating an avatar of a real person requires their explicit consent.

In Italy the reference authority is the Garante per la protezione dei dati personali (garanteprivacy.it), which publishes practical guides.

AI-generated content: transparency

The EU Artificial Intelligence Regulation (AI Act) sets out transparency obligations in Article 50, applicable from 2 August 2026 (with a transition period until 2 December 2026 for some obligations of providers of generative systems already on the market). In short, for people who use AI tools in their business:

  • deepfakes (images, audio or video that realistically depict people, places or events and could appear authentic) must be disclosed as artificially generated or manipulated, with exceptions and lighter requirements for clearly artistic, satirical or creative works;
  • AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed, unless it has undergone human review with editorial responsibility;
  • systems that interact with people (such as chatbots) must make it clear that people are talking to an AI, when this isn’t obvious.

The AI Act also requires (since 2 February 2025) that those who use AI systems in a professional context take measures to ensure a sufficient level of AI literacy among their staff.

Tip. Even when the law doesn’t require it, transparency pays off: a short note such as “Images created with the help of artificial intelligence” or “Text written with the support of AI tools and reviewed by [name]” protects your customers’ trust. For the regulatory details, see the official text of Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and the European Commission’s guidelines.

Quality and verification of information

The most frequent risk isn’t legal but one of quality: publishing or selling wrong information. Always apply this protocol:

  1. Identify the verifiable facts in the text (numbers, dates, names, regulations, prices, quotes, technical, health, legal or tax advice).
  2. Check them against primary sources: official websites, legal texts, tool documentation, publications from recognized bodies.
  3. Note the source and the date of the check.
  4. Flag uncertainty when sources disagree.
  5. Have specialist content reviewed (health, law, tax, finance, safety) by a professional in the field.
  6. Update products when information changes.

What we learned

  • Scams on the “AI and income” theme have recognizable signs.
  • Copyright protection requires human creative input; other people’s trademarks and works are off-limits.
  • The GDPR requires caution with personal data in AI tools.
  • The AI Act requires transparency on deepfakes, chatbots and some public-interest texts from 2 August 2026.
  • Verifying information is a procedure, not an option.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Believing promises of guaranteed income.
  • Selling products featuring protected brands or characters.
  • Entering clients’ personal data into consumer tools.
  • Publishing realistic generated content without disclosing it.

Taxes, Independence from Tools and the Future

Tax and regulatory questions to look into

Any income, even small, can have tax and social security consequences. The rules change from country to country and over time: this section gives you the questions to ask a professional, using Italy as an example.

Questions to ask your accountant

  1. Is my business occasional or habitual? (The deciding factor is continuity and organization, not just the amount.)
  2. Do I need to register for VAT or as self-employed? With which activity code?
  3. Which tax regime is suitable (for example the flat-rate regime in Italy)?
  4. Which social security contributions must I pay, and to which scheme?
  5. How is VAT handled on sales to consumers in other EU countries (the OSS scheme) and outside the EU?
  6. How do I declare income from foreign platforms (Etsy, Amazon, Gumroad, Payhip)?
  7. Do the platforms report my data to the tax authorities? (In the EU, the DAC7 directive requires digital platforms to report the data of certain sellers.)
  8. Which costs can I deduct (subscriptions, equipment)?

The example of Italy (general information, November 2026)

  • Occasional self-employment (prestazione occasionale): the oft-quoted €5,000 annual limit concerns the obligation to register with INPS’s Gestione Separata (the social security scheme) for occasional self-employed workers; it is not a threshold that exempts you from registering for VAT. An organized, continuous activity (for example an online store with a stable catalog and repeat sales) can be considered habitual even below that figure.
  • Flat-rate regime (regime forfettario): revenue limit of €85,000 a year; substitute tax of 15%, reduced to 5% for the first five years of new businesses that meet the requirements; taxable income is calculated by applying to revenue a profitability coefficient that depends on the ATECO activity code. Social security contributions are paid separately.
  • E-commerce: selling digital products is a commercial activity with specific VAT rules.

Warning. This information is simplified and only meant to orient you. Thresholds, rates and interpretations change: consult an accountant, a tax assistance center or your trade association before you start selling. If you live outside Italy, look for the equivalent information from your country’s tax authority.

Other regulatory aspects

  • Consumer rights: pre-contractual information, the right of withdrawal (with the exceptions for digital content), guarantees.
  • Advertising: the obligation to make advertising recognizable (for example in collaborations with brands on social media).
  • Professional liability insurance: consider it for consulting, automation and managing company accounts.

How to avoid depending entirely on AI tools

Tools change their prices, rules and quality. Platforms change their algorithms. A robust business reduces this dependence.

  1. Transferable skills. Invest in what stays valid with any tool: understanding customers, writing well, designing, selling, verifying.
  2. More than one tool for every critical task. Know at least one alternative for writing, design and video.
  3. Your own assets. Keep your prompts, templates, style guides and product source files in your own archives (with backups).
  4. Your own audience. Your email list and direct customer contacts don’t depend on an algorithm.
  5. Several sales channels, once the first one works.
  6. Documented processes. If a tool disappears, you must be able to rebuild your work.
  7. Human value. Relationships, responsibility, taste and experience can’t be replaced by a subscription.

How to stay up to date

The opposite risk to dependence is the anxiety of chasing every new release. A simple system:

Frequency Activity Time
Weekly Read 1–2 reliable newsletters on AI and your market 30 minutes
Monthly Check news, prices and terms of the tools you use 1 hour
Monthly Try one new feature on a real task 1 hour
Quarterly Review subscriptions, platform rules and regulatory obligations 2 hours
Yearly Update the products you sell with new information Varies

Recommended (primary) sources: official blogs and release notes from AI providers; platform help centers (Etsy, KDP, Meta, TikTok, YouTube); the European Commission’s website for the AI Act; the Garante privacy; the Agenzia delle Entrate and INPS (or their equivalents in your country).

The future: what to expect

Without making predictions that can’t be verified, some trends are already visible at the end of 2026:

  • AI agents increasingly able to carry out multi-step tasks (searching, filling in, organizing) under human supervision.
  • Integration of AI into all everyday work tools.
  • More rules and more transparency: content labeling, platform obligations, attention to copyright.
  • Saturation of generic content and greater value for what is authentic, specific, verified and signed by real people.

The guiding principle of this book remains valid in this scenario too: use AI to multiply your value, not to replace it.

What we learned

  • Before selling, you need clear answers on occasional vs. habitual activity, VAT registration, tax regime, contributions and VAT.
  • In Italy, the €5,000 limit concerns occasional self-employed workers’ INPS contributions, not an exemption from VAT registration.
  • Transferable skills, your own assets and your own audience reduce dependence on tools.
  • A regular update routine avoids both obsolescence and new-release anxiety.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Starting to sell without clarifying your tax position.
  • Believing there are no obligations below a certain amount.
  • Depending on a single tool or a single channel.
  • Chasing every new release without a method.

Glossary

Affiliate marketing — A system in which you earn a commission on sales of other people’s products generated through your link.

AI Act — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on artificial intelligence. It sets rules for providers and users of AI systems in the European Union, including transparency obligations.

ATECO (code) — In Italy, the classification of economic activities used for VAT registration; among other things it determines the profitability coefficient in the flat-rate regime.

Automation — A sequence of actions that software performs automatically when an event (trigger) occurs.

B2B / B2C — Business to Business (selling to companies) / Business to Consumer (selling to consumers).

Carousel — A social media post made of several images or slides to swipe through.

Chatbot — A program that converses with people by text or voice.

Context window — The maximum amount of text a model can consider at one time.

Conversion rate — The percentage of people who take the desired action (for example buying) out of all visitors.

Copywriting — Writing copy with a commercial goal.

DAC7 — An EU directive requiring digital platforms to report the data of certain sellers to the tax authorities.

Data processor — Under the GDPR, an entity that processes personal data on behalf of another (the controller).

Deepfake — Audio, video or image content generated or manipulated with AI that realistically depicts people, places or events and may appear authentic.

Domain — A website’s address (for example businessname.com).

EPUB — The standard ebook format, which adapts to screen size.

Flat-rate regime (regime forfettario) — In Italy, a simplified tax regime for self-employed people with revenue below a threshold, with a substitute tax.

Funnel (customer journey) — The stages a person goes through from discovery to purchase and loyalty.

GDPR — The European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2016/679).

Generative AI — Artificial intelligence that creates new content: text, images, audio, video, code.

Hallucination — An AI response containing false information presented as true.

Hook — The opening line or image of a piece of content that grabs attention.

Landing page — A web page with a single goal (for example collecting contacts or selling a product).

LLM (Large Language Model) — A large language model, the technology behind assistants such as ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude.

Marketplace — A platform where many sellers offer products to a shared audience (Etsy, Amazon).

Merchant of record — The entity that formally sells to the end customer and handles payments, taxes such as VAT and refunds on the creator’s behalf.

Mockup — An image showing a product in a realistic context (for example a planner on a desk).

MVP (Minimum Viable Product) — The minimum version of a product, good enough to be tested and sold.

Niche — A specific market segment with shared needs.

OSS (One Stop Shop) — An EU system that simplifies declaring and paying VAT on sales to consumers in other EU countries.

Print on demand — Printing a product only when it’s ordered.

Prompt — A written (or spoken) instruction given to an AI tool.

Royalty — The percentage of the price paid to the author for each copy sold.

SEO — Search engine optimization: practices for appearing in search results.

Tag — Keywords attached to a listing or content to make it easier to find.

Template — A predefined model to customize.

Token — A unit of text (a word or part of a word) processed by a language model.

Trigger — The event that starts an automation.
